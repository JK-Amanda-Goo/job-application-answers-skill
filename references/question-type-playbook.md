# Question type playbook

Application questions fall into a handful of recognizable types, and each rewards a different structure. Using a behavioral structure on a motivational question is one of the most common ways a well-written answer still lands flat.

Identify the type first, then pick the shape. If a question spans two types (common — "why this role, and what would you bring to it?"), handle both parts rather than defaulting to whichever is easier.

---

## 1. Motivational — "Why us / why this role?"

**Asking:** have you actually thought about *us*, or are you mass-applying?

This is the question where generic answers are most obvious and most fatal. Every applicant says the mission resonates.

**Shape:** specific hook → why it connects to their actual trajectory → what they'd do here.

- **The hook must be checkable.** A product decision they have an opinion on, a thing they've used, a problem in the space they've worked on, something specific about the company's approach. Not the mission statement, which everyone read on the same page.
- **Connect to their real path**, not to flattery. The strongest version makes the application look like an obvious next step rather than an aspiration.
- **Forward-looking close** — a concrete sense of what they'd work on, showing they read the JD.

**Fails when:** it praises the company in ways that would apply to any competitor. Test by swapping in a rival's name — if it still reads fine, it's not answering the question.

---

## 2. Behavioral — "Tell us about a time you..."

**Asking:** evidence you've actually done this, plus how you think.

**Shape:** compressed situation → what *they* specifically did → outcome → what it changed in how they work.

- **Keep setup short.** Context deserves a sentence or two. The interesting part is the decision, and applicants routinely spend half their words on background.
- **First person, unambiguous.** "We" throughout makes the personal contribution unreadable.
- **The tension is the story.** What was genuinely hard, what the tradeoff was, what they chose against. An account with no difficulty in it isn't an answer to this question.
- **Outcome with a number** where one exists. If there isn't one, say what changed concretely.
- **Brief reflection** — what they'd do differently, or what it changed in their approach. One line; more becomes padding.

**For failure/conflict/weakness variants:** pick something real. A disguised strength ("I care too much about quality") is transparent and reads as evasive — it fails the question more decisively than an actual failure would. What's being tested is self-awareness, so the reflection carries more weight here than the outcome does.

---

## 3. Case / technical — "How would you approach X?"

**Asking:** how do you think, and do you understand this problem?

**Shape:** frame the problem → approach → tradeoffs → what you'd need to know.

- **Reframe before solving.** Naming what's actually being asked — and what's ambiguous — often scores higher than the solution.
- **Show the tradeoff.** Any real approach costs something; naming that is the mark of someone who's done it.
- **State assumptions explicitly**, and what you'd validate first.
- **Ground in their own experience** where possible: "when I did something similar at X, the thing that broke was Y."

**Fails when:** it's a plausible-sounding framework with no substance underneath. The hiring manager knows this domain and reads generic structure as a lack of real experience.

---

## 4. Background — "Tell us about yourself / walk us through your path"

**Asking:** is there a coherent story here, and does it point at this role?

**Shape:** through-line → two or three waypoints that support it → why that leads here.

- **Lead with the through-line**, not chronology. "I've spent six years making complicated tools usable by people who aren't experts" organizes a career; "I started at X, then moved to Y" makes the reader do the work.
- **Select, don't summarize.** They have the resume. Pick what's load-bearing for *this* role.
- **Address the obvious question** if there is one — a pivot, a gap, a nonlinear jump. Briefly and without apology. Leaving it unaddressed makes the recruiter guess.

---

## 5. Open-ended — "Anything else we should know?"

**Asking:** usually nothing in particular — it's a free slot.

Two ways to use it well:

- **Fill a gap in the application.** The strongest use: something the resume can't show, or context for a question a screener will otherwise have to ask (work authorization, a pivot, a relocation).
- **Add a genuine dimension.** Something real about how they work or what they build, if it's actually distinctive.

**Leave it short.** Two or three sentences. This field rewards restraint — a long answer to "anything else?" reads as not knowing when to stop.

Skipping it is acceptable when there's genuinely nothing. Padding it is worse than leaving it blank.

---

## 6. Logistics — visa, notice period, salary, location

**Asking:** exactly what it says.

Answer directly, in one or two sentences, no framing or justification. These are filters, not evaluations, and hedging on a factual question creates doubt where none existed.

If the honest answer is a complication (needs sponsorship, three-month notice, not currently local), state it plainly with whatever makes it workable. Ambiguity here reads worse than the constraint itself.
