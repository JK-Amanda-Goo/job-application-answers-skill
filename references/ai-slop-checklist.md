# AI-slop checklist

Concrete tells that writing came out of a language model. Read the draft against these and fix what you find.

Why this is worth a dedicated pass: recruiters read a high volume of application answers and have gotten fast at spotting generated text. An answer that trips these patterns doesn't just read as bland — it reads as *not written by the applicant*, which costs more than bland would.

The underlying issue in almost every case is the same: the model reaches for a fluent generic phrase where a specific one was available. The fix is usually to go more specific, not to reword.

## 1. Manufactured enthusiasm

Stated excitement is worthless — everyone applying claims it. Demonstrated interest is the whole point.

Cut or replace:
- "I'm thrilled / excited / genuinely excited to..."
- "I'm deeply passionate about..."
- "I would love the opportunity to..."
- "It would be an honor to..."

Replace with something that *shows* the interest: a specific thing about the product they've used, a decision the company made that they have an opinion about, a problem in the space they've already worked on. "I've been running your API in a side project since March and hit the rate-limit design problem firsthand" says more than any adjective.

## 2. Buzzwords and corporate filler

Each of these is a word that survived because it sounds professional while carrying almost no information:

`leverage` · `spearheaded` · `seamless` · `cutting-edge` · `robust` · `synergy` · `holistic` · `impactful` · `best-in-class` · `game-changing` · `world-class` · `passionate` · `dynamic` · `innovative` · `utilize` (just say "use")

Also watch: `drive` and `deliver` as all-purpose verbs, `space` for industry, `journey` for career.

Test: if the sentence stays equally true after deleting the word, delete it.

## 3. The rule of three

Models default to three-item lists constantly — three adjectives, three responsibilities, three reasons. Once you notice it, it's everywhere, and it makes prose feel generated.

> "I bring curiosity, rigor, and empathy to every project."

Two is fine. Four is fine. One well-chosen thing is often best. What you want to avoid is *every* list in the piece having exactly three items.

## 4. "Not just X, but Y"

Along with its relatives: "It's not about X — it's about Y," "More than just X," "X isn't enough; Y is what matters."

This construction is a model signature. It also usually smuggles in a false contrast, since X and Y are rarely actually opposed.

Just assert Y.

## 5. Uniform rhythm

Human writing varies. Model writing tends toward sentences of similar length and identical structure, often all subject-verb-object, often all 15–25 words.

Read the draft aloud (or count clause lengths). If every sentence has the same shape, break it up. A three-word sentence after a long one does more for readability than any word choice.

Also watch for two or three consecutive sentences opening the same way — especially a run of "I did X. I did Y. I did Z."

## 6. Abstract self-praise

The most common failure. Claims with no anchor:

> "I have a proven track record of driving impactful results in fast-paced environments."

Nothing here is checkable, so a reader discounts all of it. Every claim about the applicant should be attached to something concrete — a number, a named thing, a specific decision, a timeframe.

If a sentence describes the applicant but couldn't be falsified, it's not doing work.

## 7. Formulaic openings and closings

Openings to avoid:
- "As a [role] with X years of experience in [field]..."
- "Throughout my career, I have consistently..."
- "When I first learned about [Company]..."

Closings to avoid:
- "I would welcome the opportunity to discuss further."
- "I am confident I would be a valuable addition to your team."
- "Thank you for considering my application." (in a *form field* — this isn't an email)

Application answers can start on the substance. The frame is usually deletable.

## 8. Punctuation tells

- **Em dashes** — a couple in a 200-word answer is fine; five is a signature.
- **Semicolons** — models overuse them for balanced clauses that read as machine-generated.
- **Rhetorical questions** — "So what did I learn?" reads as filler.
- **Bold sprinkled through prose** for emphasis in a plain-text form field.

## 9. Hedging

`arguably` · `perhaps` · `it could be said that` · `in many ways` · `to a certain extent` · `I believe I can`

Hedges make an applicant sound uncertain about their own experience. They did the thing; state that they did it.

## 10. Explaining the obvious

Models pad by restating the question or defining common terms:

> "Product management is fundamentally about balancing user needs with business goals, and in my experience..."

The reader wrote the question and works in the field. Cut straight to the answer.

---

## Sanity check before you finish

Read the answer and ask: **could another applicant for this same role have submitted this, changing only the name?**

If yes, the specifics aren't specific enough — go back for a real detail. That question catches more slop than any individual item on this list.
